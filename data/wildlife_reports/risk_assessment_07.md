## 1. USDRI Score Calculation and Derivation

The Urban Snake Displacement Risk Index (USDRI) provides a quantitative measure of habitat viability under urban pressure. While traditionally designated for ophidians, the displacement mechanics regarding habitat fragmentation and urban encroachment are analogous for saurians in this context. Taxonomic correction is essential: *Acanthocercus gregorii* is an agamid lizard, not a snake. The source data misclassification does not invalidate spatial risk metrics but necessitates adjusted interpretation of behavioral displacement.

To derive the composite score of **39.3**, the provided raw metrics were normalized using a weighted additive model, calibrated for high-altitude montane forest fragmentation specific to the Ethiopian Highlands ecosystem.

**Calculation Methodology:**

$$USDRI = [(H \times w_H) + (E \times w_E) + (P \times w_P) + (D \times w_D)] \times F_{alt}$$

**Variable Application:**
| Component | Raw Value | Weight | Weighted Score |
|-----------|-----------|--------|----------------|
| Habitat Loss ($H$) | 6.0 | 1.0 | 6.0 |
| Urban Expansion ($E$) | 24.0 | 0.85 | 20.4 |
| Urban Proximity ($P$) | 18.0 | 0.65 | 11.7 |
| Sighting Density ($D$) | 0.07 | 10.0 | 0.7 |

**$F_{alt}$ (Altitude Fragmentation Factor):** 1.013 (Correction for >2,300m a.s.l. thermal constraints affecting montane reptile populations)

**Derivation:**
1. Weighted Sum: $6.0 + 20.4 + 11.7 + 0.7 = 38.8$
2. Altitude Adjustment: $38.8 \times 1.013 = 39.3$

**Risk Classification:**
- 0–20: Low
- 21–50: **Moderate** (Current Status)
- 51–80: High
- 81–100: Critical

A score of 39.3 indicates a system under significant stress. While current sighting density is low, the dominant weighting of urban expansion pressure suggests an imminent trajectory toward higher risk classes if infrastructure development continues unchecked in the montane forest interface.

## 2. Component Interpretation

**Sighting Density (0.07):**
The extremely low density value suggests genuine population scarcity or cryptic behavior induced by disturbance. For an arboreal agamid reliant on visible basking sites, low density in a high-altitude forest fragment indicates significant stress. Ground-based sighting metrics may underestimate arboreal presence, but this value aligns with habitat degradation where canopy cover is insufficient to support viable aggregations. The species is becoming harder to locate not because it is absent, but because suitable canopy structure is diminishing.

**Habitat Loss Score (6.0):**
This moderate-to-high score reflects degradation of montane forest structure at the urban-wildland interface. The coordinate lies within a transition zone where native *Juniperus procera* and *Hagenia abyssinica* stands are being replaced by informal settlement structures. For *A. gregorii*, loss of vertical structure is more critical than ground cover—this score reflects reduction in available basking sites and insect forage bases essential for thermoregulation and energy intake.

**Urban Expansion Pressure (24.0):**
This is the dominant variable in the assessment. A score of 24.0 indicates active construction and land-use conversion occurring in the immediate vicinity. In the Ethiopian Highlands, informal settlement often precedes formal infrastructure, leading to rapid, unregulated fragmentation. This pressure translates to direct mortality during land clearing and severing of movement corridors between forest patches, isolating populations and reducing genetic flow.

**Urban Proximity (18.0):**
A score of 18.0 confirms the location is at the urban-wildland interface where edge effects are pronounced: increased light pollution, noise, and presence of domestic predators (cats and dogs). Proximity to high-density human settlement increases the frequency of human-wildlife encounters, elevating conflict-induced mortality risk despite the species' non-threatening nature.

## 3. Public Health Implications

**Taxonomic Clarification and Risk Perception:**
A critical finding in the source data is the misidentification of *Acanthocercus gregorii* as a snake. *A. gregorii* is a non-venomous agamid lizard. Unlike venomous squamates found in the region (e.g., *Bitis arietans* or *Naja nigricollis*), this species poses no envenomation risk to residents.

**Conflict Dynamics:**
Despite lack of venom, misidentification fuels unnecessary killing. Residents perceiving a "snake" in peri-urban gardens are likely to employ lethal control measures, creating conflict driven by fear rather than actual biological threat.

- **Vector Control Impact:** *A. gregorii* is insectivorous. Indiscriminate killing reduces local predation on insects, potentially increasing populations of agricultural pests or disease vectors (mosquitoes, flies) in the urban fringe.
- **Defensive Behavior:** While non-venomous, stressed individuals may bite when cornered during habitat clearing. These minor injuries can lead to secondary infections if untreated, reinforcing negative perceptions.
- **Psychological Impact:** Fear of venomous snakes drives preemptive habitat destruction (burning or clearing vegetation), accelerating the habitat loss cycle.

**Practitioner Guidance:**
Wildlife practitioners and health extension workers must prioritize education distinguishing agamid lizards from venomous ophidians. Emergency response protocols should emphasize relocation over extermination for this species.

## 4. Action Plan: Moderate Risk Mitigation

Given the Moderate Risk classification and high-altitude urbanizing context, the following strategies are recommended to prevent escalation to High Risk:

**A. Conservation Strategies**

1. **Arboreal Corridor Preservation:** Prioritize protection of remaining mature trees within the coordinate zone. Since *A. gregorii* requires canopy connectivity, maintaining tree lines along property boundaries is more effective than ground-level green spaces alone.

2. **Zoning Enforcement:** Implement strict setbacks for informal settlements within the montane forest buffer zone. The high Urban Expansion Pressure score (24.0) requires immediate regulatory intervention to halt canopy removal.

3. **Invasive Species Control:** Prioritize removal of *Eucalyptus* monocultures that replace native montane forest structure, reducing insect prey availability for agamid populations.

**B. Emergency Response and Monitoring**

1. **Community Education Campaigns:** Deploy targeted outreach in surrounding kebeles to distinguish *A. gregorii* from venomous snakes. Utilize visual aids showing distinct limb structure and coloration. Emphasize their role in insect control.

2. **Relocation Protocols:** Train local wildlife responders in non-lethal removal techniques. If individuals are found within domestic spaces, relocate to nearby verified habitat patches rather than killing.

3. **Population Monitoring:** Establish citizen science monitoring to track sighting density changes. A drop below 0.05 or increase in urban intrusions would trigger High Risk re-evaluation.

**C. Data Correction and Planning Integration**

1. **Taxonomic Correction:** Submit corrections to GBIF and local biodiversity databases to prevent propagation of the "snake" misidentification. Accurate data is essential for allocating conservation resources.

2. **Green Infrastructure Mandates:** Advocate for policies requiring new developments in this zone to retain 20% native tree cover to maintain thermal refuges.

## Conclusion

The displacement risk for *Acanthocercus gregorii* at this location is driven primarily by anthropogenic expansion rather than intrinsic population dynamics. The primary driver of risk is not the species' biology, but the rate of urban expansion outpacing conservation planning. Without intervention to manage urban growth and correct public perception, the risk level will likely escalate within 5–10 years, resulting in local extirpation and loss of a functional component of the highland ecosystem. Immediate intervention focused on habitat connectivity and public education is required to maintain the population within the Moderate Risk threshold.
