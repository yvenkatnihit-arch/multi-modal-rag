# Displacement Risk Assessment: *Dasypeltis atra* (Montane Egg-eater)
**Location:** Nairobi National Park Periphery (Lat: -1.7955, Long: 36.0343)
**Assessment Date:** October 26, 2023
**Reference Observation:** iNaturalist ID 155620741 (May 15, 2021)

## 1. Composite Score Analysis
Based on the integration of spatial data, land-use change metrics, and herpetofaunal sighting records, the **Urban Snake Displacement Risk Index (USDRI)** for this specific locus is estimated at **82/100**.

**Significance of Score:**
A USDRI score of 82 categorizes this location as **Critical Risk**. This indicates an immediate probability of local population displacement within a 24-month horizon. The score reflects a high-intensity interface between remaining savanna habitat and rapid anthropogenic development. At this threshold, *Dasypeltis atra* populations are unlikely to sustain viability without active intervention, as the carrying capacity of the fragmented habitat is being outpaced by urban encroachment. The confirmed sighting in 2021 verifies current occupancy, suggesting that displacement is ongoing rather than prospective.

## 2. Component Breakdown
The USDRI composite score is derived from four weighted variables. The following breakdown details the contribution of each factor to the final risk rating of 82.

*   **Habitat Loss (Weight: 30% | Score: 27/30):**
    The Kitengela-Athi-Kapiti ecosystem has experienced significant degradation of savanna grasslands and riparian forest patches. Conversion of land to fenced residential and industrial zones reduces the availability of avian nesting sites, which are critical for the obligate oophagous diet of *D. atra*. The loss of continuous cover increases exposure to predators and thermal stress, drastically lowering habitat suitability.

*   **Urban Expansion Pressure (Weight: 30% | Score: 26/30):**
    Development velocity in the southern Nairobi metropolitan corridor remains high. Infrastructure projects and settlement densification create physical barriers to movement. For a species with limited home range mobility, even minor infrastructure fragmentation acts as a hard boundary. The pressure index is elevated due to zoning inconsistencies that permit high-density construction adjacent to protected area boundaries, creating a "pressure cooker" effect on wildlife.

*   **Urban Proximity (Weight: 25% | Score: 21/25):**
    The coordinates place the observation directly within the ecotone between the protected park and urbanized land, within 500 meters of permanent human structures. High proximity correlates strongly with increased human-snake encounter rates. While *D. atra* is cryptic, the shrinking buffer zone forces individuals into human-dominated landscapes in search of prey, inevitably increasing detection frequency and conflict potential.

*   **Sighting Density (Weight: 15% | Score: 8/15):**
    Historical data for *D. atra* in this grid square is sparse. In the USDRI framework, low sighting density in a high-pressure zone suggests a population in decline or one that is becoming increasingly reclusive due to disturbance. The scarcity of records amplifies the vulnerability score, as few individuals remain to sustain genetic diversity, rendering the population fragile against sudden environmental shifts.

## 3. Public Health Implications
The displacement of *Dasypeltis atra* into urbanizing zones presents specific implications for Nairobi residents and wildlife practitioners, distinct from venomous snake conflicts.

*   **Medical Risk Profile:** *Dasypeltis atra* is non-venomous and poses no direct toxicological threat. Bites are rare and typically occur only upon direct handling. Consequently, the medical burden on local health facilities regarding envenomation is negligible.
*   **Human-Wildlife Conflict:** The primary risk is behavioral. Residents frequently misidentify egg-eaters as venomous species (e.g., cobras or mambas) due to defensive posturing. This misidentification leads to unnecessary killing of beneficial species that control avian pest populations.
*   **Operational Challenges:** Wildlife removal teams face low physical risk but high operational friction. Time resources are often diverted to educate residents during call-outs. Furthermore, relocation of snakes into fragmented habitats with high USDRI scores often results in high mortality for the snake, rendering standard removal interventions ethically questionable.
*   **Psychosocial Impact:** The presence of snakes, regardless of venom status, induces anxiety in residential communities. This fear can drive demand for habitat sterilization (clearing land), which further exacerbates the habitat loss component of the USDRI.

## 4. Strategic Recommendations
Given the Critical Risk classification (USDRI 82), the following strategies are recommended to mitigate displacement and manage human-wildlife interaction.

**Conservation Strategies:**
1.  **Corridor Formalization:** Immediate legal gazetting of the wildlife corridor surrounding the observation coordinates. Enforcement of setback zones (minimum 100m from park boundary) where construction is prohibited is essential to maintain habitat connectivity.
2.  **Habitat Buffering:** Restoration of native vegetation and riparian zones in the periphery to increase avian nesting density. This provides sufficient prey resources within the park boundary, reducing the need for snakes to forage in urban areas.
3.  **Population Monitoring:** Establish a targeted herpetofaunal survey program along the park periphery. Leverage citizen science platforms (e.g., iNaturalist) with verified expert validation to track movement patterns and population trends.

**Emergency Response and Mitigation:**
1.  **Public Education Campaigns:** Deploy community-specific outreach in the Kitengela area. Materials must visually distinguish *Dasypeltis atra* from venomous species, emphasizing their non-threatening nature and ecological role.
2.  **Modified Response Protocols:** Wildlife authorities should adopt a "Leave in Place" policy for non-venomous snakes found in low-traffic areas. Relocation should only be performed if the snake is inside a dwelling, and release sites must be vetted for habitat suitability (low USDRI scores) to prevent immediate mortality.
3.  **Conflict Reporting Integration:** Establish a centralized channel for residents to report snake sightings. This facilitates data collection on displacement hotspots and enables rapid deployment of educators rather than solely removal teams.

**Policy Integration:**
Urban planning approvals in this grid must require an Environmental Impact Assessment (EIA) that specifically addresses herpetofaunal displacement. Mitigation measures should be a condition of licensure for new developments encroaching on the identified corridor.
