# Displacement Risk Assessment: *Chamaeleo dilepis* (Mombasa Coastal Zone)

**Reference Record:** GBIF ID 5140009733 | **Date:** April 8, 2025
**Location:** Mombasa, Kenya (Lat: -4.6475, Lon: 39.3828)
**Risk Classification:** Moderate (USDRI Composite: 28.0)

## 1. Score Interpretation and Component Analysis

The Urban Snake Displacement Risk Index (USDRI) composite score of 28.0 classifies the current displacement risk for *Chamaeleo dilepis* in this sector of Mombasa as **Moderate**. While this value does not indicate an immediate crisis threshold, it reflects a destabilizing trajectory driven primarily by anthropogenic landscape modification rather than population density. The score suggests that while the species is not yet ubiquitous within the urban matrix, the underlying environmental pressures are accelerating.

The risk profile is defined by the following four contributing components:

*   **Urban Expansion Pressure (26.0):** This is the dominant driver within the index, contributing the majority of the risk weight. The high score indicates aggressive infrastructure development and land-use conversion in the immediate vicinity of the observation record. In the context of Mombasa's coastal growth, this metric signals that habitat compression is occurring faster than natural adaptation rates, effectively removing the buffer zone between wild habitats and human settlements.
*   **Urban Proximity Score (17.0):** This metric confirms that sightings are occurring within significant proximity to established urban centers. A score of 17.0, coupled with the expansion pressure, suggests a shrinking buffer zone between remaining forest fragments and residential/commercial zones. This proximity directly correlates with the reported increase in human-reptile encounters.
*   **Habitat Loss Index (6.5):** While lower than the urban pressure metrics, this index reflects specific degradation of critical microhabitats, namely mangroves and coastal forests. The value indicates that while total cover may not be entirely obliterated, the *quality* and *connectivity* of the habitat are compromised. This fragmentation isolates populations, making them more vulnerable to local extinction events.
*   **Sighting Density (0.62):** This relatively low density is critical to interpret correctly. In isolation, it suggests low abundance. However, within the USDRI framework, a low sighting density coupled with high urban pressure often indicates **cryptic displacement**—the species is likely retreating into remaining fragments rather than thriving in the urban matrix, making detection difficult until local extirpation occurs.

**Secondary Metrics Context:** The Fragmentation Index (1.71) reinforces the Habitat Loss data, indicating patchy remaining vegetation. The Population Density (3.91 units/km²) is consistent with the sighting density, suggesting sparse distribution. The Nearest Hospital Distance (4000m) is relevant for response logistics but less critical given the species' toxicological profile.

## 2. Public Health Implications

The public health implications of *Chamaeleo dilepis* displacement in Mombasa must be differentiated between direct medical risk and socio-behavioral conflict.

*   **Direct Medical Risk:** *Chamaeleo dilepis* is non-venomous. Consequently, the risk of envenomation is nonexistent. The primary medical concern is secondary bacterial infection resulting from bite wounds if the animal is mishandled or provoked during an encounter. Additionally, like many reptiles, chameleons can act as vectors for zoonotic pathogens such as *Salmonella* spp. The Nearest Hospital Distance of 4000m is sufficient for routine care but highlights a potential response lag in emergency scenarios involving vulnerable populations.
*   **Psychological and Behavioral Impact:** The primary public health burden is psychological. Increased visibility of reptiles in residential zones often triggers ophidiophobia or general herpetophobia, leading to panic-induced injuries or unnecessary killing of the animal. Misidentification as a venomous species is a common complication in urban settings, exacerbating conflict.
*   **Wildlife Practitioner Safety:** For removal teams and ecological practitioners, the risk involves physical injury during capture (bites, claw scratches) and potential exposure to ectoparasites. The moderate risk level suggests that while encounters are increasing, standard personal protective equipment (PPE) remains mandatory.

## 3. Action Plan: Conservation and Emergency Response

Given the "Moderate" risk classification, the strategy must balance immediate conflict mitigation with long-term habitat preservation. The goal is to prevent the risk level from escalating to "High" as urban expansion continues.

### Conservation Strategies
1.  **Habitat Corridor Enforcement:** Utilize the Fragmentation Index (1.71) to identify critical linkage zones between mangrove patches and coastal forests. Advocate for zoning regulations that protect these corridors from further Urban Expansion Pressure. Implement arboreal connectivity structures where necessary to reduce road mortality.
2.  **Public Education Campaigns:** Deploy targeted awareness programs in the specific Mombasa sub-locations corresponding to the Urban Proximity Score. Focus on dispelling myths regarding venomosity, promoting coexistence, and emphasizing the ecological role of *C. dilepis* in pest control.
3.  **GBIF Data Integration:** Continue monitoring Sighting Density trends. A sudden spike in this metric would indicate population compression rather than growth, necessitating a reassessment of the risk level.
4.  **Urban Green Infrastructure:** Mandate the retention of native vegetation in new developments to provide micro-refugia, reducing the necessity for long-distance displacement.

### Emergency Response Protocols
1.  **Capture and Relocation:** Establish a standard operating procedure for local wildlife officers. Specimens found within residential zones should be captured using non-invasive tools (e.g., soft-capture noose poles) and relocated to verified habitat patches with suitable carrying capacity.
2.  **Incident Reporting:** Standardize data collection for all human-chameleon encounters. Records should include GPS coordinates, habitat type, and human activity context to refine future USDRI calculations.
3.  **Medical Guidance:** Distribute guidelines to local clinics regarding the treatment of reptile bites (wound cleaning, tetanus status verification) to ensure consistent care, despite the low severity of injuries.
4.  **Threshold Monitoring:** Set a trigger mechanism. If the Composite Score exceeds 35.0 or Urban Expansion Pressure exceeds 30.0, escalate to "High Risk" protocols, which would include active habitat restoration and increased patrol frequency.

**Conclusion:**
The current USDRI score of 28.0 reflects a system under stress. While the non-venomous nature of *Chamaeleo dilepis* mitigates severe public health outcomes, the driving forces of habitat loss and urban expansion are active and intensifying. Without intervention in urban planning and habitat preservation, the composite score will likely escalate. Immediate coordination between conservation bodies and municipal planning authorities is required to stabilize the human-wildlife interface in this coastal zone.
