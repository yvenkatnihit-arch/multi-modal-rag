### Urban Snake Displacement Risk Assessment: *Philothamnus punctatus*
**Location:** Mombasa, Kenya (Lat: -4.6474, Lon: 39.3839) | **Date:** 2025-04-01 | **Source:** iNaturalist (Obs ID: 268931140)

---

#### 1. Score Interpretation
The composite Urban Snake Displacement Risk Index (USDRI) score of 28.0 classifies the current displacement risk for *Philothamnus punctatus* in the specified Mombasa locality as **Moderate**. Within the 0–100 USDRI framework, this value indicates a transitional state where the species remains viable but is experiencing measurable anthropogenic stress. While the score does not yet reflect a critical emergency threshold (typically >60.0), it signals a destabilizing trajectory. The Moderate classification serves as a proactive warning rather than a retrospective summary; it highlights a window of opportunity for mitigation before habitat fragmentation becomes irreversible. Failure to address the underlying drivers will likely result in escalation to High Risk status within 24–36 months given current coastal development rates.

---

#### 2. Component Analysis
The composite score is derived from four primary weighted vectors, each revealing specific pressures on the local *P. punctatus* population:

*   **Urban Expansion Pressure (26.0):** This is the dominant driver of risk in the current assessment. The high value reflects aggressive infrastructure development and land-use conversion in the coastal fringe. This metric captures the velocity of urbanization, indicating that habitat fragmentation is accelerating. As construction encroaches on peripheral green spaces, available territory for foraging and thermoregulation shrinks, forcing snakes into higher-density pockets or direct contact with human settlements.

*   **Urban Proximity Score (17.0):** This metric quantifies the decreasing buffer zone between remaining habitat patches and human dwellings. A score of 17.0 indicates that sightings are increasingly occurring within or immediately adjacent to residential zones. This proximity elevates the likelihood of human-snake encounters, directly correlating with the reported increase in human-reptile interactions.

*   **Habitat Loss Index (6.5):** While numerically lower than expansion pressure, this index confirms ongoing degradation of critical mangrove and coastal forest ecosystems. The destruction of these specific biomes removes essential arboreal cover and prey base for *P. punctatus*. The score suggests that while total habitat loss is not yet absolute, the quality and connectivity of remaining habitat are compromised.

*   **Sighting Density (0.62):** This relatively low density indicates the species is not ubiquitous across the urban matrix. However, in the context of high expansion pressure, low sighting density may indicate cryptic behavior driven by disturbance rather than absence. It implies the population is retreating into remaining refugia, making them less visible but potentially more vulnerable to localized extinction events if those refugia are cleared.

*   **Secondary Covariates (3.91, 1.71):** These additional factors contribute minimally to the composite score but likely represent environmental stressors such as pollution levels or microclimate alterations that subtly degrade habitat quality without causing immediate displacement.

#### 3. Public Health Implications
Although *Philothamnus punctatus* is non-venomous and poses no direct toxicological threat to humans, the displacement trends identified in this assessment carry significant public health and ecological implications:

*   **Misidentification and Conflict:** In coastal Kenya, green snakes are frequently misidentified as venomous species (e.g., *Dendroaspis* spp.). As habitat loss forces snakes into residential areas, encounter rates will rise. Public fear often leads to indiscriminate killing, posing physical risks to residents attempting to dispatch snakes and creating a cycle of conflict that undermines community safety.

*   **Ecological Health Services:** *P. punctatus* plays a role in controlling insect and small vertebrate populations. Removal of this predator through conflict-related killing can lead to secondary pest outbreaks, potentially increasing vector-borne disease risks associated with rodents or insects. Their presence often indicates rodent activity, which carries genuine zoonotic risks.

*   **National Health Context:** Kenya reports approximately 4,000 snakebite-related deaths annually according to WHO estimates, placing it among the highest-burden countries for snakebite envenomation in sub-Saharan Africa. While this species is non-venomous, increased displacement of non-venomous snakes frequently precedes or accompanies increased encounters with venomous species as the broader snake community is displaced from deteriorating natural habitat.

*   **Psychosocial Impact:** Increased sightings in urban areas can generate community anxiety and reduce perceived safety of outdoor spaces. Managing this perception is as critical as managing the biological risk.

#### 4. Action Plan
To maintain the risk level at "Moderate" and prevent escalation, the following conservation and response protocols are recommended:

**Conservation Strategies:**
*   **Habitat Corridor Protection:** Immediate designation of protected buffer zones around remaining mangrove patches and coastal forests. Development permits within 500 meters of these cores should require mandatory ecological impact assessments focusing on herpetofauna.
*   **Urban Planning Integration:** Collaborate with county planning authorities to enforce setbacks from critical ecological zones. Green infrastructure should be mandated in new developments to maintain connectivity.
*   **Population Monitoring:** Establish a longitudinal tracking program using the USDRI framework to monitor changes in sighting density and habitat loss quarterly. If Urban Expansion Pressure increases by >10% in the next assessment cycle, the Action Plan must shift from mitigation to emergency intervention.

**Emergency Response Protocols:**
*   **Relocation Over Removal:** Train local wildlife officers and fire services in safe capture and relocation techniques. Snakes should be relocated to verified habitat corridors rather than killed. Relocation sites must be vetted to ensure they are outside high-density residential zones but within suitable ecological parameters.
*   **Public Education Campaigns:** Deploy targeted community outreach programs to distinguish *P. punctatus* from venomous species. Emphasize the ecological benefit of the Spotted Green Snake to reduce fear-based killing. Utilize local community leaders to disseminate information.
*   **Reporting Hotline:** Establish a dedicated channel for residents to report sightings, building on the iNaturalist data stream. This allows for rapid response by trained personnel and feeds into a real-time risk map for responders.
*   **Medical Preparedness:** Ensure local clinics are aware that while bites are non-venomous, wound care protocols (cleaning, tetanus prophylaxis) should be standard to prevent secondary infection. Residents should familiarise themselves with the location and operating hours of the nearest hospital with antivenom treatment capacity.

---

The current data indicates a precarious balance. Immediate adherence to these protocols is necessary to ensure the coexistence of Mombasa's urban expansion and its native herpetofauna.
